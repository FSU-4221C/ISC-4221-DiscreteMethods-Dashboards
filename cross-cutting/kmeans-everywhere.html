    One loop, four steps

    • ● Slides, M5
    • ● Tutorial
    • ▣ Lab 10, Lab 11

    M5 states the algorithm as four numbered steps and calls it k-means, or Lloyd's method. Every appearance of it elsewhere in the course is these same four steps with the nouns swapped.

    1. Initialise. Choose k points c1…ck, called the centres or generators.
    2. Assign. Give every data point to the nearest centre. The set of points nearest cj is cluster Cj.
    3. Move. Replace each centre by the average of the points that were assigned to it.
    4. Test. If the centres have stopped moving, stop. Otherwise go back to step 2.

    Step 3 is not a guess. M5 proves a small lemma first: for a set of numbers, the value that minimises the sum of squared distances to all of them is their average. That is why moving a centre to the mean is the right move, and it is why the whole loop is a descent on one energy.

    The energy every version minimises (M5 topic 5.4.1)

    E = ½ one half ∑j=1k  ∑xi ∈ Cj ‖cj − xi‖2

    k
    how many centres you asked for — you choose this, the algorithm does not
    cj
    the jth centre, generator, or representative shade
    Cj
    every data point closer to cj than to any other centre
    xi
    one data point: a record, a pixel's grey level, or a sample of the region
    ‖ ‖
    the distance function, Euclidean unless the problem says otherwise
    The termination test (M5 topic 5.4.5)

    maxj ‖cj(t+1) − cj(t)‖ ≤ tolerance

    t
    the iteration number
    tolerance
    a small number you choose; the loop also needs a maximum iteration count as a safety net

    Key idea

    Each pass through steps 2 and 3 lowers the energy, and there are finitely many ways to assign the points, so the loop always stops. It does not follow that it stops at the best answer — only at one it cannot improve by moving a single centre.

    The same symbols in three places

    Read this table across a row and you are reading one idea in three dialects. Read it down a column and you are reading one module's version of the whole algorithm.

    The shared structure of k-means across M4, M5 and M6

    One algorithm, three appearances: what each symbol means in colour quantization, in clustering, and in centroidal Voronoi tessellation
    Shared idea M4 §4.3.4–4.3.5 — colour quantization M5 §5.4 — clustering, Lloyd's method M5 §5.5 and M6 — centroidal Voronoi tessellation
    The data point xi one pixel's grey level, an integer from 0 to 255 one record, a point in d dimensions one sample of the region — the region has infinitely many points, so you sample it
    The centre cj one of the k shades the printer can actually produce a cluster centre a generator zi of the Voronoi diagram
    Step 2, assign each pixel takes the nearest available shade each record joins the nearest centre's cluster each sample falls in the Voronoi cell of its nearest generator
    Step 3, move each shade becomes the mean grey level of the pixels using it each centre becomes the centroid of its cluster each generator moves to the centre of mass of its cell
    Step 4, stop when no pixel changes shade no centre moves further than the tolerance zi equals zi*, the cell's centre of mass — which is the definition of a CVT
    Weights how many pixels share that grey level importance of a record — weighted k-means, topic 5.4.9 a density ρ over the region
    What you get the same picture in k shades, so it compresses k clusters and their centres a CVT — with a hexagonal lattice in the interior of a uniform region
    Named there as K-means quantization, colour depth reduction k-means, Lloyd's method Lloyd's algorithm for a CVT; the sampled version is MacQueen's or probabilistic Lloyd's

    Definition

    Voronoi cell
    Every point of the region that is closer to generator zj than to any other generator. This is step 2 of k-means, stated geometrically.
    Centroidal Voronoi tessellation
    A Voronoi diagram in which every generator already sits at the centre of mass of its own cell. This is the fixed point of the k-means loop, stated geometrically.

    Note

    M5 says it outright at the end of the geometric clustering section: for discrete data, computing a CVT is clustering, and the algorithm is Lloyd's. The two halves of M5 — statistical clustering and geometric clustering — meet at exactly this sentence.

    Cost, initialisation and choosing k

    What it costs

    Cost of Lloyd's method (M5 topic 5.4.7)

    cost = O(k × records × iterations)

    k
    number of centres
    records
    number of data points — pixels, records, or samples
    iterations
    how many times the loop runs before it converges; not known in advance

    The work is dominated by step 2: for every data point, compute the distance to every centre and take the smallest. That is why quantizing an image is expensive — the record count is the pixel count — and why M5 points forward to computational geometry for faster ways to find the nearest generator. Compare this row against the rest of the course on the complexity comparator.

    Initialisation decides which answer you get

    Common mistake

    Treating the output as the clustering. k-means is guaranteed to converge and is not guaranteed to converge to the global minimum: the same data with different starting centres can settle on different clusterings with different energies. The standard remedy is to run it several times from different starts and keep the lowest energy — not to trust one run.

    The demo above exposes this directly: change the initialisation control from “first k data points” to a random start, change the seed, and read the final energy in the summary. If two runs on the same data give two different energies, you have just reproduced topic 5.4.6.

    Choosing k

    Nothing in the algorithm chooses k. M5's advice is to run it for several values and watch the total cluster variance: it always falls as k rises, so you look for the elbow — the point after which adding another centre buys very little. On the four-group data used by the demo, the elbow sits at three to five.

    How k is chosen in each of the three settings

    What fixes k in each appearance
    Setting What k is Who chooses it
    Colour quantization the number of shades available the hardware — 32 greys on the printer, 16 colours in the compressed image
    Clustering the number of groups you believe are there you, guided by the elbow in the variance-versus-k plot
    Centroidal Voronoi tessellation the number of generators, so the number of cells you, from the resolution the mesh or the sampling needs
