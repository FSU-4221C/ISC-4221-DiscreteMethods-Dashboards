    Delaunay triangulation

    M5 meets it as a by-product of the Voronoi diagram — join each generator to its neighbours and a triangulation falls out. M6 meets it as the answer to a completely different question: of the many ways to triangulate a point set, which one avoids thin triangles? They are the same object, and Lab 13 is where it does real work.

    Key idea

    Both doors open on the same room. Every Voronoi diagram has exactly one Delaunay triangulation attached to it, and for points in general position that triangulation is also the one that maximises the smallest angle. Which definition you use depends on what you already have in your hands: a set of cells, or a set of points.

    M5's door: the Voronoi dual

    • ● Slides, M5
    • ● Tutorial

    M5 introduces the Voronoi diagram as a way of clustering a continuous region: each generator owns every point closer to it than to any other generator, and the boundary between two neighbouring cells is the perpendicular bisector of the line joining their generators. Connect each generator to its neighbours and you have the Delaunay triangulation.

    The smallest honest example has three generators. Their three perpendicular bisectors meet at one point — the circumcentre of the triangle they form — and the Voronoi diagram is three rays leaving it.

    The last row is the whole duality in one number: the Voronoi vertex is equidistant from all three generators, which is exactly what it means for the circle through A, B and C to contain no other generator. That is the empty-circle property, and it is the reason the two definitions agree.

    The Voronoi half of this thread is also the k-means half — see k-means everywhere, where the assign step of Lloyd's method turns out to be a Voronoi diagram and the converged state is a centroidal Voronoi tessellation.

    M6's door: maximise the smallest angle

    • ● Slides, M6 part 3
    • ▣ Lab 13

    M6 starts somewhere else entirely. Draw lines between points without ever crossing one you have already drawn; when you can draw no more, every point is a vertex of a triangle. Do it again and you get a different picture — there are many triangulations of the same points. So which one do you want?

    If the edges are roads you might want the shortest total length. If the triangles are patches of territory you probably dislike very thin ones. For graphics and for numerical work, avoiding small angles is the usual criterion, and that gives the definition.

    Delaunay by exhaustion (M6 topic 6.5.3)

    θ(T) ≤ θ(T*)   for every triangulation T of the same points

    T
    any triangulation of the point set
    θ(T)
    the smallest angle occurring in any triangle of T
    T*
    the Delaunay triangulation — the one whose smallest angle is as large as it can be. Usually, but not always, unique

    Common mistake

    Reading this as “Delaunay has no thin triangles”. It says only that no other triangulation of these points does better. If the points themselves are badly placed, the best available smallest angle can still be tiny — and on the Lab 13 sensor field it is 0.08 degrees. The guarantee is comparative, not absolute.

    Note

    The definition is also degenerate when the points are cocircular — four corners of a square, for instance. Then both diagonals give the same smallest angle and there is no unique Delaunay triangulation. That is why the demo below places its points on an ellipse rather than a circle.

    Carry the definition out

    • ✚ New for 2026

    Every triangulation, measured and ranked

    The definition says “consider all possible triangulations”, which is normally where a lecture stops. For points in convex position the count is a Catalan number — 5, 14, 42 and 132 for five to eight points — so the demo can actually build all of them, measure every angle, and sort them worst first. Stepping to the last one lands on the Delaunay triangulation, by definition rather than by algorithm.

    Lab 13: a field from scattered sensors

    • ▣ Lab 13
    • ◐ Slides

    Lab 13 is where the triangulation stops being a picture. 120 air-quality sensors are scattered over a rectangular region Ω = [0, 10] × [0, 6] kilometres. Each reports a pollutant concentration. The task is to build a Delaunay triangulation of the sensor positions, fit a linear function over each triangle from its three vertex readings, and integrate to get the total pollutant load.

    The field being sampled (Lab 13, Problem 2)

    f(x, y) = 20 e−[(x−5)2 + (y−3)2]/3 + 4 sin(1.5x) + 3 cos(2y)

    x, y
    position in kilometres, with 0 ≤ x ≤ 10 and 0 ≤ y ≤ 6
    the exponential term
    a pollution plume centred on (5, 3)
    the sine and cosine terms
    smaller regional variation

    Over each triangle the interpolant is f ≈ a + bx + cy, whose three coefficients are fixed by the three vertex readings, so the gradient (b, c) is constant inside the triangle. That is first-order finite-element interpolation, and it is the whole reason the shape of the triangles matters.

    Common mistake

    Blaming the 9 per cent error on the quadrature rule. It is not a quadrature error at all — the Delaunay mesh only covers the convex hull of the sensors, which is 89.3 per cent of Ω, so roughly a tenth of the region is never integrated. Refining the rule will not fix it; only extending the domain will. The shipped reference solution reports this same error and calls it “small, indicating good approximation quality”, which is worth arguing with.

    Key idea

    Two claims that sound alike but are not: Delaunay maximises the minimum angle among triangulations of this point set is true and is the definition; Delaunay avoids skinny triangles is false, and the histogram you produce in Lab 13 disproves it on your own data.

    Three questions worth answering before you submit

    1. Your estimate is about 9 per cent below the exact value. Is that a quadrature error or a domain error? Name the number from your own output that settles it.
    2. Your minimum-angle histogram has a triangle at well under one degree. Does that contradict the claim that Delaunay avoids thin triangles? Answer in one sentence, using the definition above.
    3. The scenario says you only have sensor readings. Given that, is f at the triangle centroid something you can actually evaluate — or do you only have the vertex average?
