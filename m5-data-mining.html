    Module 5

    Data Mining

    Two halves of one problem. Clustering takes data with no labels and finds the groups that are already in it. Classification takes data that is labelled and learns a rule that labels the next record. Everything in between — distance functions, energies, dendrograms, Voronoi cells, impurity — is machinery for deciding what "similar" means.

    5.1 Introduction

    5.1.1 What is data mining?

    # Permalink to topic 5.1.1, What is data mining?
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    "Data mining" started life as an insult. Statisticians used it for the practice of running a dataset through enough tests that something eventually looks significant — dredging until the data confesses. The course opens with that history on purpose, because the warning it carries is still live: a powerful tool applied without statistical judgment produces confident nonsense.

    Definition

    Data mining
    The extraction of implicit, previously unknown, and potentially useful information from data.

    Each clause of that definition does work. Implicit — it is not a database query; the answer is not already written down. Previously unknown — rediscovering what you already believed is not a finding. Potentially useful — a true pattern that nobody can act on is a curiosity, not knowledge.

    Why now? The lecture's estimate is that the amount of information in the world doubles roughly every 20 months, and that the number and size of databases grows faster still — from photos, video, phone calls, texts, email and social posts; from sensors and the internet of things (water meters, oil pipelines, the power grid); from satellites, meteorology, simulation output, financial markets, and medical and genomic testing. Almost none of it will ever be seen by human eyes. If it is going to be understood at all, it will be understood by computers.

    Common mistake

    The cautionary tale the course tells: parapsychologist J. B. Rhine asked students to guess the colours of ten cards. About one in a thousand got all ten right — and declared them psychic. But ten fair coin flips agree by chance with probability (0.5)10 = 1/1024, so one in a thousand is exactly what randomness predicts. On retest the "psychics" did no better than anyone else. Rhine's conclusion was that being told you have ESP causes you to lose it. The real lesson is that a pattern with no baseline is not a pattern.

    5.1.5 Historical case: John Snow and cholera

    # Permalink to topic 5.1.5, Historical case, John Snow and cholera
    • ● Slides
    • ◐ Tutorial, partial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    London, 1854. Cholera killed most of its victims within three days, and nobody knew why. Germ theory did not exist yet; the accepted explanation was miasma — bad air. Miasma theory even fit the evidence loosely, because outbreaks clustered in poor districts full of tanneries and butcher shops that smelled terrible.

    Dr John Snow suspected the water pump on Broad Street. He had no way to see a pathogen, so he needed a geometric argument instead. What he did amounts to constructing a Voronoi diagram by hand:

    John Snow's four evidence-gathering steps

    Snow's four evidence-gathering steps, and what each one corresponds to in the algorithm
    Step What Snow did The Voronoi equivalent
    1Drew a street map of the districtDefine the region S
    2Marked every house where a cholera victim had livedThe sample points
    3Paced out the walking distance from each house to the nearest pumpsThe distance function d
    4Drew a line around the houses closest to the Broad Street pumpThe Voronoi cell of one generator

    Deaths piled up densely inside the Broad Street cell and thinned out sharply beyond it. Snow then personally interviewed the exceptions — the brewery workers inside the cell who stayed healthy because they drank beer rather than water; the sick children who lived outside the district but attended school inside it; the woman living miles away whose son delivered Broad Street water because she liked its taste. Every exception, once explained, strengthened the conclusion.

    Key idea

    Snow's map destroyed miasma theory because it showed where deaths occurred in a way that suggested why. That is the entire promise of clustering: a partition of space by nearest generator, read as an explanation. The formal machinery arrives in 5.5.1, Voronoi diagrams.

    5.2.1 What is clustering?

    # Permalink to topic 5.2.1, What is clustering?
    • ● Slides
    • ▣ Lab 10
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Clustering strives to identify groups of objects that behave similarly or show similar characteristics — the lecture notes call them look-a-like groups. Similarity is not a vague notion here: it is quantified by a distance function, and the choice of that function is a modelling decision that changes the answer.

    Definition

    Clustering
    Partitioning a set of objects into groups so that objects within a group are more similar to each other, under a chosen distance function, than they are to objects in other groups. No group labels are supplied in advance.
    Unsupervised learning
    The machine-learning name for the same activity: there is no "teacher", no correct answer supplied with the data.
    Segmentation
    The name market research uses for it.

    Clustering finds patterns in data that may be discrete or continuous, numerical or symbolic. Genetic sequence data — strings over an alphabet, with no natural coordinates — is the course's standard reminder that "distance" need not mean "distance in the plane".

    5.2.3 Distance functions

    # Permalink to topic 5.2.3, Distance functions
    • ● Slides
    • ▣ Lab 10
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    For every application we have to say what "close" means. In ℝn the default is the Euclidean distance:

    Euclidean distance in n dimensions

    d(p, q) = √( (q1−p1)2 + (q2−p2)2 + ⋯ + (qn−pn)2 )

    p, q
    two points in ℝn
    pi, qi
    their i-th coordinates
    n
    the number of attributes in a record

    It is not the only choice. The infinity (max) norm, the city-block or Manhattan norm — 'cityblock' in scipy's pdist — and application-specific metrics are all used, and Lab 10 asks you to swap one in.

    Distance between two clusters is a separate decision, and it has to be, because clusters are frequently not points at all: the colours in an image, all American-made SUVs, the set of amino-acid codons like ATG. Whatever function you pick, the course requires it to satisfy four properties.

    Definition

    Let A, B and C be clusters, each of which may consist of a single object or many. The distance d(A, B) must satisfy:

    (i) Self
    d(A, A) = 0
    (ii) Commutative
    d(A, B) = d(B, A)
    (iii) Non-negative
    d(A, B) ≥ 0. Note that d(A, B) = 0 does not necessarily mean A = B.
    (iv) Triangle inequality
    d(A, B) + d(B, C) ≥ d(A, C)

    Common mistake

    The triangle inequality points the way it does because going via B can never be a shortcut. Prof. Peterson's original lecture notes print it reversed (≤ instead of ≥); the slide deck has it right. Read it as: "the direct route is never longer than the detour."

    5.2.4 Unusual / custom distance functions

    # Permalink to topic 5.2.4, Unusual and custom distance functions
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial

    The four axioms in 5.2.3 are the only requirement. Nothing says a distance has to come from coordinates. The lecture's example is a word ladder: given two words of the same length, a path from one to the other changes a single letter at a time, and every intermediate string must itself be a word. The distance is the length of the shortest such path.

    The GOLF to WORD word-ladder path

    The shortest word-ladder path from GOLF to WORD: four single-letter changes, so d(GOLF, WORD) = 4
    Step Word Letter changed
    0GOLF—
    1GOLD4th: F to D
    2COLD1st: G to C
    3CORD3rd: L to R
    4WORD1st: C to W

    So d(GOLF, WORD) = 4, while d(GOLF, WOLF) = 1. This function satisfies all four metric properties, and none of the algorithms in this module care that it has nothing to do with geometry.

    Note

    Sometimes you have no distance function at all — only a table of pairwise similarities. Every algorithm in 5.3 works directly from such a table, which is exactly why the final-exam clustering question hands you a distance matrix instead of coordinates.

    Lab 10 asks you to implement a genuinely non-standard one and see what breaks:

    Lab 10, Problem 3 — custom distance

    d(u, v) = ∑i=1m √( |ui − vi| )

    u, v
    vectors in ℝm
    m
    the number of attributes

    Common mistake

    The arithmetic mean minimises the sum of squared Euclidean distances — that is the lemma in 5.4.1. It does not minimise the sum above. So when Lab 10 asks you to drop this metric into k-means, the "average the cluster" update step is no longer the correct minimiser of your own objective. Check whether your total variance still decreases monotonically; that discrepancy is the point of the exercise.

    5.3 Hierarchical Clustering

    5.3.1 Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering

    # Permalink to topic 5.3.1, Hierarchical agglomerative clustering
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026
    • ★ Final, Q3

    Agglomerative clustering is one loop, repeated until you run out of clusters. The lecture notes spell out the N-object walkthrough that the slides skip:

    1. Start with N clusters, one object each.
    2. Find the two closest clusters and merge them. You now have N−1 clusters. Record which two merged, and at what distance — that record is the dendrogram.
    3. Compute the distance between the new two-object cluster and each of the remaining N−2 singletons, using your chosen linkage rule.
    4. Repeat from step 2.

    Termination happens either when a single cluster remains, or when a user-specified condition is met: a desired number of clusters, or a measure of their separation.

    Key idea

    The algorithm never chooses a number of clusters. It produces the whole family of nested groupings at once, and you choose afterwards by cutting the dendrogram at a height. That is the structural difference from k-means, where k is an input.

    The only thing left unspecified is step 3 — how far apart two clusters are. That is the linkage criterion, and the next three topics are the four standard answers.

    Python Agglomerative clustering with SciPy 
    import numpy as np
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

data = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4], [5, 6], [10, 11], [12, 13], [15, 16]])

# Perform single linkage hierarchical clustering
Z = linkage(data, method='single', metric='euclidean')

# Plot dendrogram
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 5))
dendrogram(Z)
plt.title('Single Linkage Hierarchical Clustering Dendrogram')
plt.show()

    Change method to 'complete', 'average' or 'centroid' and everything else stays the same. The returned Z is the merge record: one row per merge, giving the two clusters joined and the height at which they joined.

    5.3.2 Single linkage (nearest neighbor)

    # Permalink to topic 5.3.2, Single linkage, nearest neighbor
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026
    • ★ Final, Q3

    Single linkage measures the distance between two clusters along their shortest link: the closest pair of members, one from each.

    Single linkage

    d(A, B) = min d(αi, βj)   for all i = 1,…,n and j = 1,…,m

    A
    the cluster {α1, α2, …, αn}
    B
    the cluster {β1, β2, …, βm}
    d(αi, βj)
    the distance between one member of A and one member of B

    Operationally: compute the distance for every one of the n×m pairs and take the smallest.

    Common mistake

    Single linkage suffers from chaining. Because only the single nearest pair matters, two well-separated clusters get forced together if one stray object happens to sit between them. The result is long, stringy clusters that no human would have drawn. Complete linkage (5.3.3) exists to eliminate exactly this problem.

    The compensating strength is flexibility: single linkage will happily find long, curved, non-elliptical clusters that complete linkage would chop up.

    5.3.3 Complete linkage (farthest neighbor)

    # Permalink to topic 5.3.3, Complete linkage, farthest neighbor
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Complete linkage is the mirror image: the distance between two clusters is the distance between their most distant pair.

    Complete linkage

    d(A, B) = max d(αi, βj)   for all i = 1,…,n and j = 1,…,m

    A, B
    the two clusters, as in 5.3.2
    max
    taken over every one of the n×m cross-cluster pairs

    Key idea

    The similarity of two clusters becomes the similarity of their most dissimilar members. Nothing merges unless every member of one cluster is reasonably close to every member of the other — which is precisely what stops chaining, and which biases the result toward compact, roughly spherical clusters.

    Note

    The merge rule does not change between linkages. At every stage you still merge the pair of clusters A, B for which d(A, B) is minimum — it is the definition of d that changed, not the greedy step. The 2025 slide wording ("the maximum minimum distance") is garbled; the lecture notes state it correctly.

    5.3.5 Dendrogram construction and reading

    # Permalink to topic 5.3.5, Dendrogram construction and reading
    • ◐ Slides, partial
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026
    • ★ Final, Q3(b)

    A dendrogram is nothing more than a drawing of the merge record. Each leaf is one object. Each internal join is one merge, drawn at a height equal to the distance at which that merge happened. Nothing else is encoded, and in particular the left-to-right ordering of the leaves carries no meaning.

    Definition

    Dendrogram
    A tree in which the leaves are the original objects and the height of each internal node is the linkage distance at which its two children were merged.
    Cut height
    A horizontal line drawn across the dendrogram. The number of vertical lines it crosses is the number of clusters you get if you stop the algorithm there.

    That last property is the payoff of the whole method. You run the algorithm once and then read off the clustering for every possible number of clusters by sliding a horizontal line. On the slide deck's 14-point example: cut so that nearest neighbours are no more than 0.5 apart and you get 8 clusters; cut at 1.5 and you get 5.

    Key idea

    Answering "what does the y axis tell us?" is the whole skill. It is a distance, in the units of your distance function. A tall join means the two groups it connects were far apart, so a long vertical gap below a join is evidence of a genuine separation; a cluster of short joins near the bottom is a tight group.

    The demo in 5.3.6 draws the dendrogram one merge at a time, so you can watch each bar appear at its own height and see the distance matrix shrink alongside it.

    5.4 K-Means

    5.4.1 Averages as energy minimizers (+ proof)

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.1, Averages as energy minimizers with proof
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Before k-means makes sense you have to see the average as the answer to an optimisation problem rather than as a formula you were handed in secondary school.

    Lemma — the average minimises this energy

    ℰ(c, X) = ½ ∑i=1n (c − xi)2

    X
    the data {x1, x2, …, xn}
    c
    a candidate centre, any real number
    ℰ
    the energy, or cost, of choosing that centre

    Lemma. The unique c that minimises ℰ(c, X) is the average x̄x bar = (1/n)∑xi.

    Proof. Differentiate with respect to c and set the derivative to zero:

    Step 1 — first-order condition

    ∂ℰ / ∂c = ½ ∑i=1n 2(c − xi) = 0

    ∂ℰ / ∂c
    the rate of change of the energy as the centre moves

    The ½ and the 2 cancel, so ∑i(c − xi) = 0, which gives ∑ic = ∑ixi, hence nc = ∑ixi, hence c = (1/n)∑ixi. The second derivative is n > 0, so this stationary point is the unique minimum. □

    Now generalise. Instead of one centre, allow k of them, and let each point be charged only to the centre it belongs to:

    The k-means energy

    ℰ = ½ ∑j=1k [ ∑xi ∈ Cj ‖cj − xi‖2 ]

    k
    the number of clusters, an input to the algorithm
    Cj
    the j-th cluster: the set of records assigned to centre cj
    cj
    the j-th centre, also called a generator
    ‖·‖2
    squared Euclidean length

    Key idea

    This is the basis of k-means clustering, and it explains the algorithm's personality. Because the objective squares the distance, a point twice as far away contributes four times as much. That makes k-means biased toward compact, roughly spherical clusters and highly sensitive to outliers — a single distant point can drag a centre a long way.

    5.4.2 k-means / Lloyd's method

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.2, k-means and Lloyd's method
    • ● Slides
    • ▣ Lab 10
    • ● Tutorial
    • ◐ 2025 dashboard, image only

    Hierarchical clustering is the right tool when you believe the data has a family-tree relationship. k-means asks a different question: break my data into k groups, and tell me the k average values they bundle around. The lecture's motivating example is redistricting after a census — you are not interested in a family history, you need a fixed number of groups.

    Definition

    k-means problem
    Given N points X = {x1, …, xN} in ℝd and an integer k, find k points c1, …, ck minimising the energy of 5.4.1, where Cj consists of every point closer to cj than to any other centre.
    Lloyd's method
    The standard iterative heuristic for that problem: assign, average, repeat. Solving the problem exactly is intractable, so we settle for a local minimum.
    Generator
    The course's other word for a centre — the same object that generates a Voronoi region in 5.5.1.

    Key idea

    The cluster definition — "every point closer to cj than to any other centre" — is exactly the definition of a Voronoi region. The assignment step of k-means builds the Voronoi diagram of the current centres. That is why 5.4 and 5.5 are the same algorithm told twice.

    5.4.3 The algorithm step by step

    The four steps of Lloyd's method

    Lloyd's method, as the slide deck numbers it
    StepNameWhat happens
    1InitializationChoose k points {c1, …, ck} to be the initial centres or generators
    2AssignmentFor each record xi, find the centre cj with ‖xi − cj‖ smallest, and put xi in cluster Cj
    3UpdateReplace each cj by the average of all points now in cluster j
    4Convergence testIf the centres have stopped moving, stop. Otherwise go back to step 2

    After step 1 the centres are essentially arbitrary — as the lecture puts it, "we have no expectation that these cj minimize our energy; they don't even look like the centres of the clusters." Steps 2 and 3 alternate until they do.

    Pseudocode Lloyd's method, main loop 
    for niter = 1 : max_iters

    # find centers; reassign clusters
    for i = 1 : n
        find center c(j) closest to record x(i)
        n(j) = n(j) + 1

    # move centers
    move centers c(j) to the centroid of cluster j

    # stop?
    if converged
        compute total discrete cluster variance
        break

    5.4.4 Initialization strategies

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.4, Initialization strategies

    • ▣ Lab 10
    • ● Tutorial

    Step 1 is where the trouble is. The tutorial calls initialization "a critical point of weakness", and the lab spends a whole problem on it.

    Inputs and initialised quantities for k-means

    What k-means takes in and what it sets up first
    RoleSymbolMeaning
    InputkThe number of clusters
    InputxThe data {x1, x2, …, xn} to be clustered
    Initializec{c1, …, ck}, the initial guess for the centres — usually k randomly selected records from x

    Initializemax_itersThe maximum number of allowable iterations

    The strategies the course names, in increasing order of sophistication:

    1. Random records. Pick k distinct records from the data. This is the default, and the reason it beats picking arbitrary points in space is that a chosen record is guaranteed to be somewhere the data actually lives.
    2. Multiple restarts. Run the whole algorithm several times from different random starts and keep the run with the smallest total cluster variance. Lab 10 requires at least five restarts; the tutorial suggests ten.
    3. Use what you know. If the application tells you roughly where the groups should be, seed there.
    4. Quasi-random sampling. Halton sequences or Latin Hypercube sampling spread the initial centres more evenly than uniform random draws do — mentioned only in the lecture notes, but it is the principled version of strategy 1.

    Common mistake

    Lab 10 insists the initial centres be distinct. Two identical centres are not a harmless duplicate: one of them will win every tie, the other gets an empty cluster, and the average of an empty set is undefined. The lab's rule for that case is to keep the previous centre for the empty cluster.

    5.4.5 Convergence testing / termination criteria

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.5, Convergence testing and termination criteria
    • ● Slides
    • ▣ Lab 10
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Two quantities can be monitored: the energy itself, or the total discrete cluster variance.

    Total discrete cluster variance

    ∑j=1k ( (1 / nj) ∑xi ∈ Cj ‖xi − cj‖2 )

    nj
    the number of records in cluster j
    the bracketed term
    the variance of the points in cluster j about their own centre
    the outer sum
    runs over the k clusters

    The practical stopping test is that the centres have stopped moving. If cj(t) denotes centre j at iteration t:

    Termination criterion

    maxj=1,…,k ‖cj(t+1) − cj(t)‖ ≤ tolerance

    cj(t)
    the j-th centre at iteration t
    max over j
    the largest movement of any single centre this iteration
    tolerance
    a small positive number you choose; Lab 11 uses 10−2, Lab 11's CVT problem uses 0.005

    Why it converges at all — the three-part argument from the lecture notes, which the slides state as a conclusion without proof:

    1. Whenever a point is reassigned to a nearer cluster, the sum of squared distances is reduced (that is what "nearer" means).
    2. Whenever a centre is moved to the average of its cluster, the sum of squared distances is reduced — this is the lemma of 5.4.1.
    3. If no point moves between clusters, nothing changes on the next iteration, so the algorithm has converged.

    Since both steps decrease a quantity that is bounded below by zero, and there are only finitely many possible assignments, the process must terminate.

    Common mistake

    k-means is guaranteed to converge. It is not guaranteed to converge to the global minimum. Those are different statements, and confusing them is the single most common error in this section.

    Note

    Lab 10 writes the criterion with a squared norm and Lab 11 writes it unsquared. Both are legitimate stopping tests, but a tolerance of 0.005 means different things under the two, so the labs' tolerances are not comparable. State which convention you used in your report.

    5.4.6 Sensitivity to initial generators

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.6, Sensitivity to initial generators

    5.4.7 Computational cost of Lloyd's method

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.7, Computational cost of Lloyd's method
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Almost all the work is in step 2, the assignment: for each record, find the nearest of the k generators. Done by brute force that is k distance computations per record per iteration.

    Cost of Lloyd's method

    cost = 𝒪( k × (#records) × (#iterations) )

    k
    the number of generators, checked once per record
    #records
    N, the size of the dataset
    #iterations
    how many assign/update rounds until convergence — data-dependent, not known in advance

    The update step is cheaper: one pass over the data summing coordinates, so 𝒪(N) per iteration regardless of k. And note that #iterations is not a constant you control — it is why max_iters exists.

    Note

    The k factor is what computational geometry removes. A spatial data structure over the generators answers "which generator is nearest?" in far less than k comparisons, which is the connection the slide deck flags forward to M6.

    5.4.9 Weighted k-means

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.9, Weighted k-means
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    If some records matter more than others, say so. Each xi gets a weight wi, the update step becomes a weighted average (5.4.10), and the quantity being minimised becomes the weighted cluster variance.

    Weighted cluster variance

    ∑j=1k [ ∑xi∈Cj wi‖xi−cj‖2 ]  /  [ ∑xi∈Cj wi ]

    wi
    the weight, or importance, of record xi
    cj
    the centre of cluster j, now its centre of mass
    the denominator
    the total weight in cluster j, which normalises each cluster's contribution

    Each step of weighted k-means reduces this quantity, and the best clustering minimises it. As in the unweighted case, the algorithm can still get stuck at a local minimum.

    Key idea

    The lecture's motivating questions are all siting problems: where do you open a new Starbucks or McDonald's, where should a state move its capital, where does an airline put a hub? You are choosing convenient centres, and geographic location is not the only thing that matters — population density is the weight. Points here are towns, not customers.

    5.4.10 Centroid and center of mass

    # Permalink to topic 5.4.10, Centroid and center of mass
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial

    These are the unweighted and weighted versions of the same idea, and the update step of k-means is one or the other.

    Centroid — unweighted

    cj = [ ∑xi∈Cj xi ]  /  [ ∑xi∈Cj 1 ] = (1 / nj) ∑xi∈Cj xi

    nj
    the number of points in cluster Cj — the denominator counts them
    Center of mass — weighted

    cj = [ ∑xi∈Cj wixi ]  /  [ ∑xi∈Cj wi ]

    wi
    the weight of point xi; setting every wi = 1 recovers the centroid

    Definition

    Convex hull
    The smallest convex set containing all the points. Picture the points as nails in a board and stretch a rubber band around the outside of them; the shape the band takes is the convex hull.

    The centre of mass can lie anywhere within the convex hull of the data points, and never outside it — no matter how extreme the weights get.

    5.5.1 Voronoi diagrams

    # Permalink to topic 5.5.1, Voronoi diagrams
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Everything so far clustered points. Geometric clustering partitions space. The motivating question in the lecture: given the ambulance posts in a county, which post should respond to an emergency at an arbitrary location? You are not grouping the posts — you are labelling every point of the map with the post nearest to it.

    Definition

    A Voronoi diagram needs exactly three ingredients:

    A set 𝒮
    The region being partitioned. Finite or infinite: a particular triangle, the whole plane, the surface of a sphere or a torus.
    A set 𝒞 of generators
    The special points z1, …, zk that induce the diagram.
    A distance function d(xi, zj)
    How far a point of 𝒮 is from a generator. Euclidean in the plane; geodesic on a sphere.
    Voronoi set 𝒱j
    The subset of 𝒮 consisting of all points closer to zj than to any other generator.
    Voronoi tessellation, or Voronoi diagram
    The collection of all of them, {𝒱1, 𝒱2, …, 𝒱k}.

    Key idea

    For two generators in the plane, the boundary between their regions is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them — the set of points equidistant from both. Every wall in a planar Voronoi diagram is a piece of some perpendicular bisector, which is why the walls are always straight and always meet the generator-to-generator segment at a right angle.

    Cells on the outside of the generator set are unbounded: they extend to the edge of 𝒮, or to infinity if 𝒮 is the whole plane. That is not an error in your code.

    Python Voronoi tessellation of 20 random points with SciPy

    For harder domains, use existing software rather than writing your own: Stripack tessellates the surface of a sphere into pentagons and hexagons (used for climate-model grids), and Voro++ handles three-dimensional and periodic domains such as a torus. TRIANGLE by J. Shewchuk is the standard planar tool.

    5.5.2 Computing Voronoi regions by sampling

    # Permalink to topic 5.5.2, Computing Voronoi regions by sampling

    SciPy is convenient, but you should be able to build a Voronoi diagram yourself. The obstacle is that a region of the plane contains infinitely many points, and you cannot assign each of them to a generator one at a time. The fix is to sample: discretise, or "pixel-ize", the region. Choose enough sampling points and the picture is indistinguishable from the exact diagram.

    For the unit square, divide it into M×M pixels and, for every pixel (I, J):

    Pixel index to coordinates

    (X, Y) = ( (J−1) / (M−1) , (M−I) / (M−1) )

    I
    the row index, counted from the top downward
    J
    the column index, counted from the left rightward
    M
    the number of pixels along each side
    X, Y
    the coordinates of that pixel in the unit square, with Y increasing upward

    Then find C*, the generator closest to (X, Y), and assign the pixel to it. The index flip in Y is the only subtle part: pixel rows run downward, but the y axis runs upward.

    Common mistake

    Forgetting the flip gives you a diagram that is correct but mirrored vertically, and it will look plausible enough that you may not notice. Check one corner by hand: pixel (1, 1) is the top-left of the image and must map to (0, 1), the top-left of the unit square.

    5.5.4 Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT)

    # Permalink to topic 5.5.4, Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Take any Voronoi tessellation and compute the centre of mass zi* of each cell 𝒱i. In general the generator is not at that centre — the cell it produced is lopsided about it. A CVT is the special case where the two coincide.

    Centre of mass — continuous data

    zi* = [ ∫𝒱i ρ(x) x dx ]  /  [ ∫𝒱i ρ(x) dx ]

    ρ(x)
    the density at the point x; constant density gives the geometric centroid of the cell
    𝒱i
    the i-th Voronoi cell
    Centre of mass — discrete data

    zi* = [ ∑xi∈𝒱i ρ(xi)xi ]  /  [ ∑xi∈𝒱i ρ(xi) ]

    ρ(xi)
    the weight of sample point xi — compare the weighted centre of mass in 5.4.10; it is the same formula

    Definition

    Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT)
    A Voronoi tessellation in which every generator is the centre of mass of its own cell: zi = zi* for every i.

    A CVT also minimises an energy, and the resemblance to 5.4.1 is not a coincidence:

    CVT energy

    ∑i=1K ∫y∈𝒱i ρ(y) ‖y − zi‖2 dy

    K
    the number of generators
    ρ
    the density; weights are already built into the formula, which is what makes weighting easy here

    5.5.5 CVT vs. ordinary Voronoi

    # Permalink to topic 5.5.5, CVT versus ordinary Voronoi
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Put an ordinary Voronoi diagram of ten random generators next to a CVT of ten generators over the same region and three differences show up.

    Ordinary Voronoi tessellation compared with a CVT

    Ordinary Voronoi tessellation versus a centroidal Voronoi tessellation of the same region
    Property Ordinary Voronoi CVT
    Generator positionWherever you put it; visibly off-centre in its own cellExactly at the cell's centre of mass
    Cell sizesWildly uneven; slivers are commonNearly equal under a uniform density
    Cell shapes in the interiorIrregular polygonsTend to a hexagonal lattice
    EnergyNot stationary — moving generators reduces itA local minimum of the CVT energy

    Key idea

    The hexagons are the tell. Under a uniform density, the shape that minimises the CVT energy per unit area in the plane is the regular hexagon, so a converged CVT in the interior of a region looks like a honeycomb. Cells near the boundary are distorted because the boundary cuts them.

    Under a non-uniform density the cells are not equal in area: they are small where ρ is large and large where ρ is small, so every cell carries roughly the same total mass. That is the property that makes CVT useful for adaptive mesh generation and for weighted siting problems.

    Watch this happen in the 5.5.2 demo: step from iteration 0 to iteration 8 and read the "largest cell / smallest cell" ratio in the summary. It falls monotonically.

    For discrete data, a CVT is a clustering, and the algorithm that computes it is k-means. The only difference is what you are partitioning: a finite set of records, or a continuous region sampled finely enough to stand in for one.

    5.5.7 Probabilistic Lloyd's / MacQueen's algorithm

    # Permalink to topic 5.5.7, Probabilistic Lloyd's and MacQueen's algorithm
    • ● Slides
    • ◐ Tutorial, partial
    • ▣ Lab 11
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Never build the tessellation at all. Instead:

    1. Scatter a large number of random sample points over the domain.
    2. For each sample point, determine which generator it is closest to, and keep a running count of how many samples belong to each generator.
    3. Take each generator's new position to be the average of the sample points nearest to it.
    4. Repeat.

    One can show that the generators produced this way converge to the generators of a CVT. The approach is also embarrassingly parallel — each sample point can be classified independently — which is why the lecture recommends it for parallel machines.

    Note

    Lab 11 Problem 1 is exactly this algorithm: probLloyds(dims, m, a, b, maxIter, tol) on the region (0,2)×(0,2) with 100 generators, tolerance 0.005, at 10, 100 and 1000 sampling points per generator. The point of the three sampling densities is the noise floor: with too few samples the centre-of-mass estimate is noisy, the generators jitter, and the convergence test never fires.

    Key idea

    Sampling density controls accuracy, not correctness. More samples do not change where the CVT is; they reduce the Monte Carlo error in your estimate of each centre of mass, which is what lets the "generators stopped moving" test succeed.

    Definition

    Classification
    Algorithms that assign objects to one of several predefined categories.     Definition

    5.6.4 Greedy tree construction

    # Permalink to topic 5.6.4, Greedy tree construction
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    Depending on the order in which you ask the questions, the same attribute set gives many different trees. Finding the optimal decision tree is exponentially hard — the tutorial states it as NP-complete — so we settle for a suboptimal result.

    Definition

    Greedy approach
    (i) Make the best local choice among all feasible choices available at this step. (ii) That choice is irrevocable — you never go back and reconsider it.
    Inductive learning
    Building a general rule from specific examples, which is what a decision-tree learner does with the training set.

    Key idea

    "Best local choice" is doing all the work in that definition, and 5.6.7 to 5.6.9 exist to make it precise. The measure is gain: how much purer the child nodes are than the parent was.

    Greedy is the same strategy the course uses for coin change in M1 and for Dijkstra and Kruskal in M3. Here, as there, it is fast and it is not guaranteed optimal. Hunt's algorithm (5.6.5) is the recursive form of the greedy construction.

    5.6.5 Hunt's algorithm

    # Permalink to topic 5.6.5, Hunt's algorithm
    • ● Slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026
    • ★ Final, Q2(e)

    Hunt's algorithm grows a decision tree recursively, partitioning the training records into successively more similar subsets.

    Let y = {y1, …, yl} be the class labels — for the loan example just default and no default. Start with the entire training set 𝒟0 associated with the root node i = 0. As the tree grows we create nodes indexed by i, and 𝒟i is the subset of the training data associated with node i.

    Key idea — the recursion

    At node i, ask: do all records in 𝒟i belong to the same class?

    • Yes — mark i as a terminal leaf node, labelled with that class. Stop.
    • No — break 𝒟i into children:
      1. Choose an attribute test condition to partition the records into smaller subsets.
      2. Create a child node for each outcome of the test condition.
      3. Distribute the records of 𝒟i to the children according to the outcome of the test.
      Then recurse on every child.

    Everything except "choose an attribute test condition" is mechanical. That one line is 5.6.7 through 5.6.9.

    Note

    On the loan data the three-way marital split and the binary "married versus not" split both give a Gini gain of exactly 0.12, because single and divorced have identical class proportions (2 no-default and 2 default; 1 and 1).     Common mistake

    Gain systematically favours multiway splits, because more children means smaller, purer children. In the extreme, splitting on a record ID gives N pure children and maximum gain, and a useless tree. Real implementations correct for this with a gain ratio; this course flags the issue rather than fixing it, so do not read "highest gain" as "best attribute" without checking how many children the split makes.

    Hunt's recursion as stated in 5.6.5 has two holes. Both are patched by the same rule.

    The three termination cases of Hunt's algorithm

    When node i becomes a leaf, and what label it gets
    Case Condition on 𝒟i Label assigned to the leaf
    PureAll records have the same class labelThat class
    Empty𝒟i = ∅ — no record took this branchThe majority class of the training records at its parent
    IndistinguishableAll records have identical attribute values but different class labels: x1 = x2 but y1 ≠ y2The majority class of 𝒟i

    The third case is the honest limit of the method: two records that agree on every attribute and disagree on the label cannot be separated by any tree over those attributes. No further split helps, so declare a leaf and take the majority.

    Key idea

    Those three conditions are a complete termination criterion — the recursion always halts. But it is sometimes advantageous to terminate early, before a node is pure: a leaf built from three records is a claim about the world backed by three observations, and stopping early trades a little training accuracy for a model that generalises better.

    Two different trees over the same loan data

    Two decision trees over the same ten loan records. Both classify all ten correctly
    Tree Root Then Then Rules produced
    Tree 1 Home ownerMarital statusAnnual income Home owners never default; non-owners who are married never default; single or divorced non-owners default above 80K

    Key idea

    Two trees can be equally right about the training data and disagree about a record neither has seen. That is the sense in which the greedy construction is "suboptimal": it commits to one of an exponentially large family, and gain is the heuristic that picks which one.

    This observation is the doorway to ensemble methods. Rather than trusting a single tree, grow many — each on a different random subset of the records and attributes — and have them vote. A random forest is exactly that, and it usually beats any single deep tree, at the cost of the interpretability that made trees attractive in the first place (5.6.15).

    5.6.13 Neural networks: the artificial neuron

    # Permalink to topic 5.6.13, Neural networks, the artificial neuron
    • ◐ Slides, lecture notes only
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    The biological analogy is loose but load-bearing. A human brain has on the order of 1010 neurons; each receives signals through dendrites, and if the combined input is strong enough it fires down its axon to the neurons it connects to. An artificial neuron keeps three of those features: many inputs, a weight per input, and a threshold.

    The artificial neuron

    a = ∑i=1n wixi − θ   then   y = φ(a)

    xi
    the i-th input
    wi
    its weight — the model of synaptic efficacy, how strongly that input counts
    θ
    the neuron's single threshold, also called the bias when moved to the other side
    a
    the activation: the weighted sum minus the threshold
    φ
    the activation or transfer function, applied to a to give the output. A step function gives a perceptron; sigmoid and ReLU are the usual modern choices

    Key idea

    Without φ, a stack of neurons is just a stack of linear maps, and composing linear maps gives a linear map. The non-linearity of the activation function is the entire reason a network can express more than a straight line.

    Neurons are arranged in a feedforward layered topology. The tutorial's school analogy: the input layer is elementary school, receiving raw features; the hidden layers are middle and high school, learning progressively more abstract features (edges, then shapes, then objects); the output layer is the final exam, producing the prediction.

    Where they are used, from the lecture notes: detection of medical phenomena, stock market prediction, credit assignment, and monitoring the condition of machinery.

    5.6.14 Neural networks: the learning process

    # Permalink to topic 5.6.14, Neural networks, the learning process
    • ○ Not in the slides
    • ● Tutorial
    • ✚ New for 2026

    A decision tree is constructed; a neural network is trained. The architecture is fixed in advance and the weights are adjusted until the outputs are right. The tutorial gives the four steps of backpropagation:

    1. Feedforward. Push a training instance through the network and read the output.
    2. Loss calculation. A loss function quantifies how wrong that output was, given the known label.
    3. Backpropagation. Work backwards from the loss, using the chain rule, to attribute a share of the error to every weight and bias in the network.
    4. Weight update. Nudge each weight in the direction that reduces the loss.

    Repeat over thousands or millions of instances, often many times over the same training set.

    Definition — the firing rule

    The lecture notes describe learning at the level of one neuron, without calculus. A neuron is taught a collection of input patterns and the output each should produce. For a pattern it was not taught:

    Fires (output 1)
    if the pattern has more input elements in common with the nearest taught "1"-pattern than with the nearest taught "0"-pattern.
    Does not fire (output 0)
    if the reverse holds.
    Undefined
    if the two are tied.

    Key idea

    The difference between the truth table of what the neuron was taught and the truth table of how it behaves after the firing rule is applied is called the generalization of the neuron. That gap is the whole point: a network that only reproduced its training data would be a lookup table.

    Worked example from the lecture notes: a three-input neuron is taught to output 1 on the patterns 111 and 101, and 0 on 000 and 001. The firing rule then fills in the remaining four patterns.

    The taught and generalized truth tables of a three-input neuron

    A three-input neuron: what it was taught, and what the firing rule generalises to for the four untaught patterns
    Input pattern Taught output Output after the firing rule Why
    00000Taught directly
    00100Taught directly
    10111Taught directly
    11111Taught directly
    010not taught0Differs from 000 in one place, from 101 in three — nearer the taught 0
    011not taughtundefinedDiffers from 001 in one place and from 111 in one place — a tie
    100not taughtundefinedDiffers from 000 in one place and from 101 in one place — a tie
    110not taught1Differs from 111 in one place, from 000 in two — nearer the taught 1

    The lecture notes extend this to three neurons that between them recognise a "T" pattern from an "H" pattern, generalising over noisy variants of each — a miniature version of what a real image classifier does.

    This is a trade-off, not a ranking.

    Decision trees compared with neural networks

    Decision trees versus neural networks, as the tutorial sets them side by side
    Aspect Decision trees Neural networks
    Interpretability"White box" — every prediction has a traceable rule path from root to leaf"Black box" — the knowledge is distributed across millions of weights
    Expressive powerAxis-aligned splits; struggles with smooth or diagonal boundariesLearns intricate non-linear patterns given enough data and depth

    Key idea

    Pick by the requirement, not by the accuracy score. The tutorial's own test case: a loan-approval system that has to justify a refusal to a regulator. A rule like "IF credit_score < 600 AND debt_to_income_ratio > 0.5 THEN Deny" is a defence; "the network said so" is not. Where accountability matters, interpretability is part of correctness.
